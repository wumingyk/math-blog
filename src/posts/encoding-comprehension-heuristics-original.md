# ENCODING & COMPREHENSION HEURISTICS

---

## Preview First (Survey the Structure)

Begin any book or paper with a quick overview. Skim the table of contents, headings, introduction, and summary to grasp the outline before diving into details.

**Why It Works:** This "big picture first" approach (seen in methods like SQ3R's Survey step) gives your brain a framework to slot details into, improving comprehension.

**Practical Application:** Spend ~5–10 minutes per chapter on this preview; for example, read section headings and the abstract so you know the major topics in advance.

---

## Ask Questions & Set Intentions

Active readers approach text with questions in mind. Before reading a section, turn the heading into a question (e.g. "What is X?") and read to find the answer.

**Why It Works:** Having a purpose focuses your attention and engages your curiosity, which boosts comprehension and memory. It transforms reading from passive intake into an active hunt for answers.

**Practical Application:** Write down a few questions you want the material to answer. For instance, if you're reading a research paper, you might ask "What problem are they solving?" or "What are the key findings?" and then read to answer those.

---

## Active > Passive Reading

Engage actively with the text. Mark up the book with marginal notes, highlight sparingly, and paraphrase key ideas in your own words. Passive reading (just moving your eyes over text) is a recipe for low retention.

**Why It Works:** Active reading forces you to process and rephrase ideas, which creates stronger memory traces than passive re-reading. By writing notes or summaries, you're essentially teaching the material to yourself, engaging recall and deeper thinking.

**Practical Application:** Treat reading as a conversation – jot down questions, reactions, and connections to other ideas as you go. For example, after a pivotal paragraph, you might pause and note: "This parallels what I read in X's book – perhaps the same principle in action."

---

## The 20% Highlighting Rule

Highlight and underline very selectively – ideally no more than 10–20% of the text. Mark only the most important points or phrases that capture the essence.

**Why It Works:** Over-highlighting is a form of mental laziness that can hinder memory (you end up bypassing the decision of what's truly important). Research shows that students who highlight too much remember less, and setting a strict limit (e.g. one sentence per paragraph) yields better retention. Focused highlighting forces you to evaluate and prioritize information, engaging critical thinking.

**Practical Application:** Try the rule of thumb: for each page, highlight at most a few key sentences. If you find you want to highlight everything, it's a sign to stop and summarize instead. This way when you review, the marked parts truly represent the core ideas.

---

## Layered Reading (Multiple Passes)

Read in layers of depth instead of one cover-to-cover slog. Do a quick systematic skim (inspectional read) to get the gist, then a slower analytical read for understanding, and a deep dive only on the most relevant or challenging parts.

**Why It Works:** Each pass builds on the last, first mapping the territory and then filling in detail. This prevents getting bogged down in minutiae before you understand the context.

**Practical Application:** For a dense article, you might first spend 5 minutes skimming headings and conclusion, then read fully but don't stop at hard parts (that's a "superficial read"), and finally re-read key sections or math proofs in depth if needed.

---

## Note Density & Paraphrasing

Keep your notes concise and in your own words. Rather than transcribing passages verbatim, write brief paraphrases or bullet points of the key idea. One expert rule of thumb: if your notes are almost as long as the text, you're not summarizing.

**Why It Works:** Paraphrasing requires you to process the meaning and re-encode it in your personal vocabulary, which dramatically improves understanding and recall. It also produces "memory hooks" more relevant to you.

**Practical Application:** After each section or concept, pause and jot a one-sentence summary from memory.

---

## Focused Attention (Eliminate Distractions)

Read in an environment optimized for concentration. Memory formation requires attention, so remove or minimize distractions: silence your phone, close unrelated tabs, and consider using a physical book or a distraction-free reader mode.

**Why It Works:** Divided attention is detrimental to encoding memories – multitasking or frequent interruptions can cause new information to never properly register in memory. Many elite readers ritualize deep-focus reading sessions (often in the early morning or late night) to get uninterrupted absorption.

**Practical Application:** Create a reading ritual: e.g., a 30-minute block in a quiet spot, with notifications off. If your mind wanders, try techniques like the Pomodoro (25 minutes reading, 5 minute break) to train sustained attention. Over time, these habits help you enter a "flow" state with texts, encoding them more deeply.

---

## Understand Before Judging

Approach new material with an open mind and delay critical thinking until you're sure you understand the author's ideas.

**Why It Works:** This is a classic Mortimer Adler rule – by suspending initial disagreement or bias, you ensure you fully comprehend the argument, which leads to better retention and a fair evaluation. If you jump to critiquing too early, you may focus on rebuttals or emotions instead of the content, missing key points.

**Practical Application:** As you read, make it a rule that you can't say "I disagree" (or agree) until you can summarize the author's position clearly. For example, if reading an investing thesis that goes against your instincts, first outline the thesis on its own terms. Only after you could explain it to someone else do you weigh in with your critique. This mindset fosters deeper engagement and memory, because you process the material fully instead of cherry-picking bits to contest.

---

## Pacing and Chunking

Break reading into manageable chunks for better processing. Rather than marathon sprints, use shorter bursts with reflection breaks. Group related ideas together into chunks (verbal, visual) to encode them.

**Why It Works:** The brain can only hold a limited amount of information in working memory at once (about 7±2 items). Chunking reduces cognitive load by grouping related items. Taking breaks allows memory consolidation – the brain strengthens neural connections during rest.

**Practical Application:** Read in 20-30 minute segments, pausing to reflect after each. Try to identify how the author groups ideas and use those natural breakpoints. After each break, spend 1-2 minutes reviewing what you just read before continuing.

---

## Elaboration & Dual Coding

Elaboration means asking "why?" and "how?" and associating new facts with existing ideas or personal experiences. Dual coding involves combining words with visuals (e.g. drawing a diagram or timeline of what you read).

**Why It Works:** Elaboration strengthens memory by embedding new information in a rich network of cues – each connection (a prior concept, a real-world example, etc.) becomes another retrieval pathway. Dual coding gives you two ways to remember (verbal and visual), doubling the chances of recall.

**Practical Application:** For every significant piece you read, try to produce at least one elaboration: connect it to something you already know, think of a real-world example, or ask why it's true. Visual learners might sketch simple diagrams, maps, or timelines. The more cues you create, the more pathways to retrieve the memory later.

---

## Spaced Repetition (Spacing Effect)

Review material at increasing intervals over time (e.g. after 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month) instead of cramming. Each review should involve active recall without looking. The gaps will reveal what didn't stick.

**Why It Works:** Each time you're about to forget, reviewing strengthens the memory. This "desirable difficulty" forces your brain to work harder, which actually helps memory – forgetting is part of learning. Cramming may get you through a test but leads to fast decay; spaced review builds long-term retention.

**Practical Application:** Schedule quick review sessions: 10 minutes after first reading, then the next day, then a few days later. Use flashcards or self-testing without looking at the source. Track what you forget and review that more often. Many readers keep a small notebook or app of key points to review during spare moments (commutes, waiting in line).

---

## Capture Thoughts Quickly

Capture thoughts quickly. Review your notes regularly and connect them. This leverages the Encoding Specificity principle – we recall information better in contexts similar to where we encoded it. By seeing ideas in networked form, you create multiple pathways to retrieve them.

**Why It Works:** The brain thrives on connection, not isolation. When you actively relate new information to old, you're building a rich knowledge network. Each connection becomes another hook to hang the memory on. This also makes your notes more useful – instead of isolated facts, you have a web of ideas that can trigger insights.

**Practical Application:** After each reading session, spend 5 minutes writing key takeaways in your own words. Then, look for connections: "How does this relate to what I learned last week?" or "What does this remind me of?" Use tools that support linking (like backlinks in note apps) to make these connections explicit. Over time, you'll build a personal knowledge base that compounds in value.

---

## Practice Testing & Retrieval

Treat testing as learning, not just assessment. After reading, force yourself to recall without looking at the text. This active retrieval is far more effective than passive re-reading for long-term retention.

**Why It Works:** Retrieval practice strengthens memory pathways – each time you recall, you're reinforcing the neural trace. It also identifies gaps in your understanding. The "testing effect" is one of the most robust findings in learning science: practicing recall dramatically improves later retention.

**Practical Application:** After finishing a chapter, close the book and write down everything you remember without looking. Then compare with the text to see what you missed. Another technique: explain the concept aloud to an imaginary student. If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough (Feynman technique). Make testing a routine part of your reading process.

---

## Focus on Reading

Set aside specific times for deep reading – perhaps early morning or late evening when your mind is fresh and distractions are fewer.

**Why It Works:** Deep work requires uninterrupted focus. When you protect this time, you can engage with complex ideas more fully. Many great thinkers and writers had specific routines for deep work – understanding your own rhythm helps you optimize your learning and creativity.

**Practical Application:** Audit your day for your peak focus hours (many people find mornings best). Reserve these for demanding reading. Protect this time fiercely – no email, no social media. Communicate to others that this is your focus time. Over time, you'll find the quality of your learning improves dramatically.

---

## Metacognition – Think About Your Thinking

Regularly step back and reflect on how you're learning. What strategies are working? What isn't? Adjust your approach based on what you discover about yourself as a learner.

**Why It Works:** Metacognition – awareness and regulation of your own thought processes – is a key predictor of academic and professional success. When you understand how you learn best, you can optimize your strategies. This self-awareness turns you from a passive consumer of information into an active director of your own learning.

**Practical Application:** After each reading session, spend 2-3 minutes reflecting: Did I understand the main points? What techniques helped me remember? Where did my attention wander? What could I do differently next time? Keep a learning journal to track patterns and refine your approach over time. The most effective learners are constantly experimenting and adjusting.
